How to interpret the gradient boosting model and the new model

The short version: - Gradient boosting has no weights to read. You interpret it from the outside, by testing what it relies on. - The new model is a logistic regression. You read it from the inside: each parameter has one number that says how strongly, and in which direction, it moves churn risk.

1. Gradient boosting: interpret it by its behaviour

How it decides

  • It's about 400 small decision trees, each one a short chain of questions such as "silent for more than 21 days? and no DNS Traffic Control? then raise the risk".
  • Each tree corrects the mistakes left by the trees before it.
  • A customer's score is the sum of all 400 trees' outputs, converted to a probability.
  • No single tree, and no single number, explains the result.

Three ways to read it

Question Tool What it tells you
What does it rely on? Permutation importance: scramble one parameter and see how much accuracy drops which parameters matter most (reports/GB_FEATURES.md)
Which direction? Partial dependence, or comparing churned and retained medians e.g. lower DTC adoption goes with higher risk
Why this customer? SHAP values (not built yet) per-account reasons, e.g. "+12 points from silence, −5 from DTC"

Cautions

  • Importance isn't impact. It says the model uses a parameter, not how much risk changes per unit.
  • Correlated parameters split the credit. DTC and RPZ share credit, so either one can look less important than it really is.
  • It finds shortcuts. In this project, gradient boosting reached a fake AUC of 0.98, and later 0.95, by exploiting data artifacts. It's powerful, so it has to be audited.
  • In v2 it wasn't significantly better than the simple model: average AUC 0.859 vs 0.842, p = 0.28. That's why it wasn't chosen.

2. The new model (logistic regression): read it directly

The formula

churn score = −2.787 + 0.628 × silence − 0.872 × DTC − 0.479 × employees
churn probability = 1 ÷ (1 + e−score)

Its parts: - silence, DTC and employees are each customer's values put on a common scale: how unusual the customer is compared with the average, in standard deviations. - Each raw value is log-transformed first, so going from 0 to 30 days of silence matters more than going from 300 to 330. - The signs give the direction: - silence is +, so more silence means more risk; - DTC and employees are −, so more of either means less risk. - The size of each number gives the strength.

The odds ratio: the easiest number to quote

Parameter Odds ratio per standard deviation In words
Silence 1.87 each step up raises the odds of churn by 87%
DTC adoption 0.42 each step up cuts the odds by 58%
Company size 0.62 each step up cuts the odds by 38%

What the numbers mean for real customers

These are calculated with the fitted model.

Customer Churn probability (next 3 months)
Reporting normally, no DTC, 1,000 employees 11%, about average
Same, but half its members on DTC 2%
Same as the first, but silent 30 extra days 37%
Silent 30 days, no DTC, 200 employees 45%
Reporting normally, full DTC, 10,000 employees 1%

This is the main advantage over gradient boosting: any customer's score can be explained with three facts.

How to use the probabilities

  • The model was fitted on a sample where about 10% of grids churn, close to reality. So "37%" roughly means about 37 in 100 customers like this would churn within 3 months.
  • Rank customers by probability and work down the list.
  • The flag cut-off (about 18% in the primary run) is a dial:
  • lower it to catch more churners, with more false alarms;
  • raise it for fewer, more certain flags.

What it does not say

  • It isn't causal. DTC users stay, but that may be because engaged customers buy DTC, not because DTC keeps them. Offering DTC trials to thin deployments would test this.
  • It only sees customers who send telemetry. The 563 churned accounts with no HEKA data are invisible to it.

3. Rule of thumb

Use Model
Decisions, dashboard scores, explaining a customer's risk Logistic model (the new model)
Benchmark: is there a pattern we're missing? Gradient boosting

If gradient boosting ever becomes significantly better than the logistic model, a new pattern exists in the data. Find it, and add it to the simple model.
